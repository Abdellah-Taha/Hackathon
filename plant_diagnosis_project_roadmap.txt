                                 PLANTCARE AI
                   Project Roadmap & Team Execution Plan
          A hackathon-ready plant image diagnosis and care assistant
PROJECT GOAL
Turn a plant photo into an understandable, evidence-aware report: identify likely visible problems, explain confidence and uncertainty, and give practical next steps. AI should do more than generate text -- it should interpret visual evidence and turn it into a structured, useful diagnosis.
                Team of 4  |  2 AI / backend  |  2 web frontend
1. Product definition
Build a web application where a user uploads a plant photo and receives a concise plant-health report. The first version should focus on a narrow, reliable workflow rather than trying to solve every agricultural problem.Core user journey
User uploads a clear image of a leaf or whole plant.
The app asks for optional context: plant name (if known), location/region, growth stage, and symptoms noticed.
The AI analyzes visible evidence and returns a structured result: likely issue, severity estimate, confidence, evidence, and recommended next steps.
The page presents the result as a readable report, including limitations and when to seek expert help.
The user can optionally ask a follow-up question about the report.
What the AI must actually do
Do not make the AI merely rewrite a template. Its core role is to inspect the image (using a vision-capable model or a dedicated plant-disease classifier), extract visual observations, compare plausible explanations, and produce a schema-validated report. The text model should explain the findings and recommendations; it should not invent a diagnosis when the image is ambiguous.MVP scope: must-have vs. later

2. Recommended technical approach
AI pipeline
1. Input validation: Check file type, size, image readability, and whether the photo plausibly contains a plant. Return a friendly error if not.
2. Visual analysis: Use a vision-capable model or a plant-disease image classifier. Ask for observable symptoms first (spots, yellowing, wilting, mildew-like growth, pest traces), not an immediate confident disease label.
3. Diagnosis hypothesis: Produce up to 1 - 3 plausible explanations with confidence/uncertainty and the visual evidence supporting each. Distinguish visible symptoms from inferred causes.
4. Context enrichment: Use user-provided plant type, season, region, and later weather data as context -- not as proof of disease.
5. Recommendation generation: Generate prioritized actions: immediate low-risk steps, what to monitor, what additional photo/context would help, and when expert advice is appropriate.
6. Structured output + validation: Return JSON that conforms to a fixed schema. Validate required fields, ranges, and allowed values before sending it to the frontend.
Important design choice: vision model vs. classifier
For a short hackathon, start with a vision-capable model API if one is available to the team and permitted by the event. It is usually the fastest route to an end-to-end demo. A dedicated classifier trained or fine-tuned on a known dataset can be more constrained and measurable, but adds dataset, preprocessing, and evaluation work. Avoid building a custom model from scratch during the event. If using an LLM vision API, verify image support, cost/rate limits, and whether its license/terms fit the event.Suggested report schema

A season label alone is weak advice. Seasonal guidance should depend on the plant type and the user's region, and should be presented as general context. For a later weather feature, request the user's location (or permission to use approximate location), call a weather API, and feed only relevant values into the recommendation step.Weather signals that can change advice
Recent and forecast rainfall: watering advice and risk of prolonged leaf wetness.
Temperature highs/lows: heat or cold stress risk.
Humidity and humidity-related conditions: potential fungal pressure, without claiming disease is confirmed.
Wind and extreme weather: plant protection and timing of outdoor care.
For the hackathon MVP, seasonality can be a short, clearly labeled context section based on plant type and user-entered region. Do not fabricate current weather or claim location-specific accuracy without an actual API response.4. Work split for four teammates

7. Quality, safety, and credibility
Use wording such as "possible cause" rather than presenting a photo-based guess as a confirmed diagnosis.
Show what the model actually observed in the image; this makes the AI role visible and the output easier to trust.
If confidence is low or image quality is poor, ask for another photo or more context instead of forcing an answer.
Avoid recommending pesticides, chemical concentrations, or irreversible actions based only on a photo. Prefer low-risk steps and suggest local expert advice when needed.
Do not claim that weather caused a disease. Explain weather as a risk factor or context only.
Protect uploaded images: avoid retaining them by default, and explain any storage or external model processing.
8. Demo script and judging narrative
Problem: plant owners notice symptoms but do not know what they mean or what to do next.
Action: upload a plant photo and optionally enter the plant type or region.
AI moment: show visual observations, ranked possible causes, confidence/uncertainty, and reasoning grounded in the image.
Useful outcome: show prioritized actions and what signs to monitor -- not just a disease name.
Future direction: add local weather signals to adapt advice to upcoming heat, rain, or humidity conditions.
9. Features to postpone
Do not spend the hackathon building user authentication, a database of every plant species, a complex RAG pipeline, model fine-tuning, chat history, or a full weather dashboard. These features can be valuable later, but they distract from proving the core experience: photo --> visual evidence --> plausible assessment --> actionable care report.10. Final checklist
